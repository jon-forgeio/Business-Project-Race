Behavior email shape for families:
1. One true positive
2. What you saw, in neutral words
3. What you have tried
4. An open question
5. One next step with a date

2.
Monday lesson opener, zero prep:

"[Last week's topic] in one word. Why that word?"

No wrong answers, so everyone can start. The "why" sentence is where the thinking shows up.

#teachers #EdChat

3.
Scholarship prompt? Underline the verb before you write a word.

Describe = a scene
Explain = how or why
Reflect = what you learned
Show why = your case

Free guide on matching your story to the prompt: https://sprintshelf.netlify.app/guides/scholarship-essay-prompt-types/

4.
Peer feedback in three moves:

Notice: "The part that stood out to me was... because..."
Ask: "I got a little lost when... Can you tell me more?"
Suggest: "One thing you could try is..."

One of each beats ten vague comments.

6.
500-word scholarship essay, rough word budget:

Hook: 75
Story: 175
What it means: 175
Looking forward: 75

The most common structure problem is the story eating the whole essay. Budget the meaning first.

7.
Tuesday opener: Spot the Mistake.

"3/4 + 1/4 = 4/8. What went wrong?"

Students find it, fix it, explain the fix. Use a misconception you have already seen in real work this week.

8.
"I don't have a story" brainstorm:

List everything you do in an ordinary week. Circle anything you chose, fixed, taught, or had to adjust.

Each circle is an essay seed.

10.
Word swaps for a calmer behavior email:

"I am concerned" -> "I have noticed"
"always" -> "a few times this week"
"refuses to" -> "has had a hard time"
"unacceptable" -> delete it

11.
The Five Whys for essay brainstorming.

Pick one thing you do. Ask "why?" five times and write each answer.

By the fifth answer you are usually near a value you actually hold. That is the heart of the essay.

Free guide: https://sprintshelf.netlify.app/guides/scholarship-essay-prompt-types/

14.
Four-part scholarship essay outline:

1. Hook: start inside a moment
2. Story: one situation, "I" plus action verbs
3. Meaning: what changed in how you think
4. Bridge: how it connects to what is next

Bullets under each part before any sentences.

16.
Helping a student with a scholarship essay? Ask, don't tell.

"What happened next?"
"How did that feel?"
"What would you do differently now?"

The story is stronger when it comes from the student.

#scholarships #parenting

17.
Before you send a hard parent email:

Did I describe what I saw, not what I think it means?
Is there one genuine positive?
Did I ask a real question?
Is there exactly one next step?

Written while frustrated? Save it as a draft until morning.

18.
Essay test before you commit to a story:

Is it mine?
Is it specific?
Does it show change?
Does it fit the prompt in one sentence?
