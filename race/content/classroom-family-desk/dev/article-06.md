# Feedback Prompts Students Can Use With Each Other

Peer feedback sounds wonderful in theory. Students read each other's work, offer thoughtful suggestions, and everyone improves. In practice, it often sounds like this: "It's good." "I liked it." "Maybe add more?"

That is not because students do not care. It is because giving useful feedback is a skill, and like any skill, it needs a structure before it becomes natural. Sentence starters give students that structure. Once they have the words, the thinking follows.

Here is a set of feedback prompts organized by purpose, plus a simple routine for using them.

## The three kinds of feedback

Before handing out any prompts, teach students that good feedback usually does one of three things:

1. **Notices what works** so the writer knows what to keep
2. **Asks a question** so the writer sees where a reader got confused
3. **Suggests one change** so the writer has a clear next step

A helpful round of feedback includes one of each. That is it. Three comments, one from each category, is more useful than ten vague ones.

## Prompts for noticing what works

These help students move past "I liked it" toward something specific.

- "The part that stood out to me most was... because..."
- "One sentence I would keep exactly as it is: ..."
- "You did a good job of [explaining, describing, organizing] when you..."
- "I could really picture... when you wrote..."
- "Your strongest evidence is..."

**Teaching tip:** Ask students to point to the exact line or section. If they cannot point to it, the comment is probably too general.

## Prompts for asking questions

Questions are often the gentlest and most useful form of feedback, because they show the writer how a reader experienced the work.

- "I got a little lost when... Can you tell me more about what you meant?"
- "What happened between... and...?"
- "Who is... ? I was not sure."
- "Why did you decide to...?"
- "What do you want the reader to feel or think at the end?"

**Teaching tip:** Remind students that a question is not a criticism. It is information. "I got lost here" helps the writer more than "This part is bad."

## Prompts for suggesting one change

Suggestions are the hardest to give kindly. These starters keep the focus on the work, not the person, and keep each suggestion small.

- "One thing you could try is..."
- "What if you moved... to the beginning?"
- "You might add an example here, such as..."
- "This part could be even clearer if..."
- "Consider cutting... because the point is already made in..."

**Teaching tip:** Limit students to one suggestion each. When writers receive five suggestions from every partner, they often feel overwhelmed and change nothing.

## A ten-minute peer feedback routine

Here is a routine that fits into a single class block.

**Minute 0 to 1: Set the focus.** Tell students what kind of feedback to look for today. For example: "Today we are only looking at whether the opening grabs the reader." A narrow focus makes feedback sharper.

**Minute 1 to 5: Read silently.** Partners swap work and read it once all the way through without writing anything. Then they read it again with a pencil.

**Minute 5 to 8: Write three comments.** One from each category, using the prompts. Comments go on a sticky note or a small feedback slip, not directly on the work.

**Minute 8 to 10: Talk it through.** Partners take turns sharing their comments. The writer's only job is to listen and say "thank you." No defending, no explaining yet.

Afterward, give writers a minute to decide which comment they will act on first. Ownership of the decision stays with the writer.

## A feedback slip you can copy

> **Feedback for:** ____________
> **From:** ____________
>
> **What works:** The part that stood out to me was ________ because ________.
>
> **My question:** I got a little lost when ________. Can you tell me more?
>
> **One thing to try:** You could try ________.

Print a stack of these and keep them in a tray. After a few rounds, many students will start using the language without the slip.

## Helping students receive feedback

Giving feedback is only half of it. Students also need practice receiving it. A few ideas:

- Teach the response "Thank you, I will think about that." It closes the exchange calmly.
- Remind writers that they get to choose which feedback to use. Not every suggestion has to be followed.
- After revising, ask writers to note which comment they acted on and what they changed. This shows feedback givers that their comments matter.

## Using these with older students and essays

These prompts work well for older students revising scholarship or application essays, too. For those, add one more question to the list: "Does the essay answer the prompt it was written for?" That question catches many problems before a final draft.

## The takeaway

Students can give each other genuinely useful feedback when they have the words for it. Notice one thing, ask one question, suggest one change. Practice that pattern a few times, and "It's good" starts to turn into something a writer can actually use.
