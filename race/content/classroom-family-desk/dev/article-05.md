# A Simple Template for Your Back-to-School Note Home

The first note you send home sets the tone for the whole year. It is the first time many families hear from you, and they will read it looking for answers to a few quiet questions: Who is this person? Will my child be okay here? How do I reach them if I need to?

A good back-to-school note answers those questions in one page. It does not need to be long or fancy. It needs to be warm, clear, and easy to scan.

Here is a section-by-section template you can adapt for almost any grade.

## Section 1: A warm hello

Open with a short welcome and a little about yourself. Two or three sentences is plenty. Share something human: a hobby, a favorite book, or what you are looking forward to this year.

**Example:**

> Welcome to [grade or class name]! My name is [your name], and I am so glad your child is in our class this year. When I am not teaching, you will usually find me [hobby]. I am especially looking forward to [one thing you are excited about this year].

Keep personal details light. A hobby or a favorite book is friendly. Your home neighborhood or family details are not needed.

## Section 2: What we will learn

Give families a short preview of the year. Skip the full curriculum. Pick three or four big ideas or units, written in plain language.

**Example:**

> This year we will:
> - Read stories from around the world and write our own
> - Learn multiplication and division strategies
> - Explore how plants and animals adapt to their habitats
> - Practice working together in small groups

Avoid jargon or standards codes. "Explore how plants and animals adapt" is clearer to most families than a list of reference numbers.

## Section 3: A typical day or week

Families like to picture what their child's day looks like. A short snapshot helps.

**Example:**

> Most days start with a quick warm-up question, followed by reading, then math. Afternoons are for science, social studies, and project time. On Fridays, we end the week with a short class meeting.

If there are routines families should know about, like a library day or a weekly folder, include them here.

## Section 4: How to help at home

This is often the section families appreciate most. Offer two or three simple, realistic ideas. Not homework assignments, just habits.

**Example:**

> A few easy ways to support learning at home:
> - Ask "What was the best part of your day?" instead of "How was school?"
> - Read together, or let your child read to you, a few times a week
> - Check the take-home folder on [day]

Keep the ideas small and flexible. Families have very different schedules, and you want every family to feel they can do at least one thing.

## Section 5: How to reach me

Be clear about the best way to contact you and when to expect a reply. Setting this expectation early saves stress for everyone.

**Example:**

> The best way to reach me is through [school messaging system or school email]. I check messages before and after school and will reply within [one school day or your school's guideline]. For anything urgent during the day, please call the school office.

Use only your official school contact channels.

## Section 6: An invitation

End by inviting families to share something about their child. This turns a one-way note into the start of a relationship.

**Example:**

> You know your child best. If there is anything you would like me to know, such as what they enjoy, what helps them learn, or anything that is on your mind, please send me a note. I would love to hear from you.

Then a simple closing: "Here is to a great year together," and your name.

## Formatting tips

- **Keep it to one page.** If it spills over, cut words before you cut sections.
- **Use headings.** Families often skim. Short headings help them find what they need.
- **Use short paragraphs and bullets.** Two to four lines per paragraph at most.
- **Check reading level.** Plain, everyday words work for more readers.
- **Consider translation.** If families in your class read in other languages, ask your school what translation support is available.
- **Read it aloud.** If a sentence is hard to say, it is hard to read.

## What to leave out

A first note is not the place for:

- A long list of classroom rules and consequences
- Detailed grading policies (save those for a separate handout or open house)
- Supply lists longer than a few lines (attach them separately)
- Anything that sounds like a warning

You want the first impression to be welcoming. The details can come later.
