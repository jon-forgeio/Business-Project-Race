# A Week of Low-Prep Lesson Openers You Can Start Monday

The first five minutes of a lesson set the tone for the next forty. When students walk in and know exactly what to do, the room settles faster and you get a moment to take attendance, hand back papers, or simply breathe.

The trouble is that "do-now" activities can eat up a lot of planning time if you build a new one every day. The fix is a simple weekly rhythm: one opener format per day, repeated every week, with only the content changing. Students learn the routine, and you only need to swap in a new question.

Here is a five-day rotation that needs nothing more than a board or a projected slide.

## Monday: One Word, One Reason

**What it looks like:** Write a topic from last week on the board. Students write one word that sums it up and one sentence explaining why they chose that word.

**Example prompt:** "Photosynthesis in one word. Why that word?"

**Why it works:** It is a gentle retrieval exercise after the weekend. There are no wrong words, so even hesitant students can start. The "why" sentence is where the real thinking happens.

**Prep time:** About thirty seconds to pick the topic.

**Quick extension:** Ask three or four students to share their word, and write them on the board. Point out how different words can capture the same idea.

## Tuesday: Spot the Mistake

**What it looks like:** Put up a short worked example, sentence, or statement with one deliberate error. Students find it, fix it, and explain the fix.

**Example prompts:**

- Math: "3/4 + 1/4 = 4/8. What went wrong?"
- Writing: "Their going to the library after lunch." Find and fix the error.
- Science: "Plants get their food from the soil." True or false? Explain.

**Why it works:** Students enjoy catching the teacher out. Finding a mistake also asks them to apply a rule, which tends to show you quickly who understands it and who is guessing.

**Prep time:** A minute or two. Common misconceptions you have already seen in student work make the best material.

## Wednesday: Would You Rather

**What it looks like:** Offer two options connected to the content. Students pick one and back up their choice with a reason from what they have learned.

**Example prompts:**

- "Would you rather live in ancient Athens or ancient Sparta? Give one reason from our reading."
- "Would you rather be a predator or prey in this food chain? Why?"
- "Would you rather solve this problem with a drawing or an equation? Explain."

**Why it works:** Midweek energy can dip, and a choice question feels like a game. The requirement to use evidence keeps it tied to the lesson.

**Prep time:** About a minute.

## Thursday: Three Things, Two Questions, One Connection

**What it looks like:** A short 3-2-1 reflection on recent learning. Students write three things they remember, two questions they still have, and one connection to something else they know.

**Why it works:** This is the opener that gives you the most information. Skim the "two questions" section while students work on the main activity and you will often find the exact thing you need to reteach.

**Prep time:** None. The format is the same every week. Just name the topic.

**Tip:** For younger students, shrink it to 2-1: two things you remember and one question.

## Friday: Picture Prompt

**What it looks like:** Project an image related to the unit, such as a map, a diagram, a photo of a landscape, or a piece of art. Students write what they notice, what they wonder, and what they think is happening.

**Example frame:**

- I notice...
- I wonder...
- I think this shows... because...

**Why it works:** Images lower the reading barrier, so students who struggle with text can still participate fully. The sentence frames give everyone a starting point.

**Prep time:** A couple of minutes to find an image you have the right to use, such as one from your textbook or an openly licensed collection.

## Making the routine stick

A few habits make this rotation run smoothly:

1. **Post the weekly schedule.** A small sign that reads "Mon: One Word, Tue: Spot the Mistake..." means students know what to expect before they sit down.
2. **Use a timer.** Five minutes, visible to everyone. When it beeps, the opener is done, even if not everyone has finished.
3. **Keep a single notebook page per week.** Students date each opener on the same page. At the end of the week, they can see their thinking side by side.
4. **Do not grade every one.** Look at them for information, not for marks. A quick check mark is plenty.
5. **Reuse your best prompts.** Keep a running list of openers that sparked good discussion. Next year, half your planning is already done.

## Sharing the routine with families

Openers can also become a nice line in your weekly update home. Something like "On Wednesdays we start class with a Would You Rather question. Ask your child which side they picked this week" gives families an easy way into a conversation about school.
