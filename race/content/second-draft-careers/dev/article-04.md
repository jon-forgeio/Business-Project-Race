# How to Pitch Yourself as a Podcast Guest Without Sounding Like a Template

Being a guest on a podcast can be a good way to share what you know, practise talking about your work, and meet people in your field. It is also a place where hosts receive a lot of pitches that are clearly copied and pasted.

The pitches that stand out tend to be the ones that show the sender actually listened, and that are about the audience rather than the guest. Here is a process for writing one.

## Step 1: Pick shows where you genuinely fit

Start small and specific. Look for shows where:

- The audience has a problem you know how to help with.
- Recent guests are people at a similar stage to you, not only famous names.
- The format suits you (interview, panel, solo with a guest segment).

A short list of well-matched shows is more useful than a long list you cannot research properly.

## Step 2: Listen before you write

Listen to at least two recent episodes of each show. While you listen, note:

- The host's name and how they open the show.
- One specific moment or idea you found useful.
- Topics they have already covered, so you do not pitch a repeat.
- Whether they take guest pitches at all. Many shows say so on their website.

That one specific moment becomes the first line of your email.

## Step 3: Frame your topic as the listener's problem

Hosts are thinking about their audience. So instead of "I would like to talk about my work in operations," try "Your listeners run small teams, and many of them struggle with handing off work when someone is out. I can walk through a simple handover routine."

A useful test: if you removed your name from the pitch, would the topic still sound worth an episode?

## Step 4: Give the host something to picture

Include two or three talking points. These help the host imagine the conversation and decide quickly. Keep each one to a single line:

1. Why the usual advice on [topic] falls short.
2. A simple method you use, in three steps.
3. One mistake to avoid, and what to do instead.

## Step 5: Keep the bio to one line

The host does not need your full history in a first email. A one-sentence bio is enough:

> "[Name] helps [audience] [result] through [method]."

Add one link where they can learn more. Past interviews, a talk, or a portfolio work well if you have them.

## Step 6: Put it together

A pitch to adapt:

> **Subject:** Guest idea for [Show]: [topic]
>
> Hi [Host],
>
> I enjoyed your episode on [episode], especially [one specific detail].
>
> I work on [your area] and could speak about [topic] for your listeners:
> 1) [talking point]
> 2) [talking point]
> 3) [talking point]
>
> A one-line bio: [bio].
>
> If it fits the show, I would be glad to send more detail. Thanks for the work you put into it.
>
> [Your name]

It is short on purpose. A host can read it in under a minute and know whether it fits.

## Step 7: Subject lines that are clear, not clever

Plain subject lines tend to do better than mysterious ones. A few patterns:

- Guest idea for [Show]: [topic]
- A fresh angle on [topic] for your listeners
- Loved your episode on [topic]; a related idea
- What [audience] gets wrong about [topic]

## Step 8: Follow up without pressure

If you hear nothing, a gentle sequence is enough:

- After about a week: "Just checking in on my note about [topic]. Happy to send a short outline."
- After another week: "One more nudge. If it is not right for the show, no problem. Thank you for your time."

Then stop. If you later have a genuinely new angle or a resource the host might like, you can send that with no ask.

## Step 9: If you are booked, make it easy for them

- Test your microphone and room in advance.
- Send any links or notes the host asks for before the recording.
- After the episode, send a short thank-you and share the episode where your audience will see it.

## Step 10: Keep a simple tracker

A single table helps you avoid double-pitching and remember follow-ups:

Show | Host | Date pitched | Angle | Follow-up 1 | Follow-up 2 | Outcome | Notes
