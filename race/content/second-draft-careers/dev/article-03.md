# LinkedIn Outreach That Does Not Sound Like Spam

Most people can tell within a sentence whether a LinkedIn message was written for them or pasted into a hundred inboxes. The pasted ones usually share a few habits: a vague compliment, a long paragraph about the sender, and a big ask from a stranger.

Good outreach is the opposite. It is short, specific, and easy to answer. Here is a way to write it, step by step.

## Step 0: Make your profile do some of the work

When someone gets your message, there is a good chance they click your name before replying. Two parts of your profile matter most in that moment:

- **The headline.** It appears next to your name in search, comments and messages. Say what you do and who you help, not only your job title. "Support operations lead helping software teams answer customers faster" tells a reader more than "Support Lead."
- **The first lines of your About section.** Only the opening shows before "see more." Lead with who you help and with what, then give proof below.

If those two things are clear, your message does not need to explain your whole background.

## Step 1: Have a real reason to write

Before you write anything, finish this sentence: "I am writing to this person because..."

Good reasons are specific:

- You read something they wrote and have a genuine question.
- You are applying for a role on their team.
- You share a former employer, school or community.
- Someone you both know suggested you talk.

"I want to expand my network" is not a reason the other person can act on.

## Step 2: Keep the connection note short

Connection notes have a tight character limit, so treat them as a single clear sentence or two:

> "Hi [Name], I read your post on [topic] and the point about [detail] matched what I saw at [Company]. I am exploring [field] roles and would like to follow your work."

No ask, no pitch. You are just giving context for why you clicked "connect."

## Step 3: Make the first real message easy to answer

Once connected, a first message should be readable in a few seconds and end with a small, specific question.

A simple structure:

1. **Context** (one sentence): why you are writing.
2. **Relevance** (one sentence): one fact about you that makes the question make sense.
3. **The ask** (one sentence): something small with a clear yes or no.

Example:

> "Hi [Name], thanks for connecting. I am applying for the [role] on your team and have spent the last few years doing [relevant work]. Would you be open to a 15-minute call next week so I can ask two questions about how the team works?"

Small asks get answered. "Can you refer me?" to a stranger is a big ask. "What does a good first 90 days look like on your team?" is a small one.

## Step 4: Avoid the common spam signals

Things that make a message read like a template:

- Opening with "I hope this message finds you well" and nothing else personal.
- A compliment that could apply to anyone ("I am impressed by your profile").
- More than one ask in the same message.
- Attachments or links in the first message to a stranger.
- Long paragraphs about yourself before you say why you are writing.

Read your draft and ask: could I send this exact message to ten other people? If yes, add one detail that only fits this person.

## Step 5: Follow up once, politely

If there is no reply after a week or so, one short follow-up is fine:

> "Hi [Name], a quick follow-up in case this got buried. No worries if now is not a good time."

After that, let it go. People are busy, and silence is rarely personal.

## Step 6: Track who you contacted

A simple list prevents double messages and missed replies. Columns that are enough:

- Name and company
- Date contacted
- Reason for writing
- Reply (yes / no / none)
- Next step and date

## Step 7: Give before you ask, when you can

Outreach goes better when it is not one-way. Share a useful article with someone you talked to last month. Congratulate a former colleague on a new role. Comment thoughtfully on a post. None of this needs an agenda.
