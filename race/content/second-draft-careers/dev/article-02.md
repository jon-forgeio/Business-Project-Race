# The First Week After a Layoff: A Calm, Day-by-Day Plan

A layoff tends to arrive with two urges at once: freeze, or apply to everything immediately. Neither works well. The first week is better spent building a base you can search from: your paperwork in order, your wins written down, your resume updated and a few real conversations started.

This plan breaks that into one focus per day. Each day is meant to take a few hours, not a full workday. Leave room to be a person.

## Day 1: Stabilise

Before anything about jobs, handle what has a deadline.

- Write down your last working day, your severance terms and the end dates of any benefits.
- Note anything you need to decide by a specific date, and put those dates in a calendar.
- Save copies of what you are allowed to keep: performance reviews, project lists, contact details for colleagues who are happy to stay in touch. Check your company's policy if you are unsure.
- List your monthly bills and mark which ones can pause, shrink or be renegotiated.
- Tell a few trusted people what happened. One sentence is enough.

If you are in a country or state with unemployment benefits, look up the official application process today. Rules and timelines vary, so use the government source rather than a summary.

## Day 2: Write down your wins

Your memory of your last job is sharpest now. Use it.

- List ten things you accomplished in your last two roles.
- For each, write what you did, what changed, and any number you can honestly stand behind (time, volume, money, people, frequency).
- Circle the five you are proudest of. These become your strongest resume bullets and your interview stories.

## Day 3: Rewrite the resume

Use the list from Day 2. For each bullet, follow a simple pattern: **verb, what, how much, result.**

- Cut "responsible for."
- Lead each role with its most relevant result.
- Keep it to a length a reader can scan. For many people with under ten years of experience, one page works.

You do not need a perfect resume. You need one that is accurate, readable and better than last week's.

## Day 4: Message ten people

Short, specific messages to people who already know you will usually go further than cold applications in week one.

A reasonable mix:

- Five former colleagues.
- A few recruiters who work in your field.
- A couple of people at companies you would like to work for.

A simple script:

> "Hi [Name], I hope you are well. My role at [Company] ended this month and I am looking for [target role]. Would you be open to a 15-minute call, or to pointing me at one person I should speak to? Happy to return the favour any time."

Send them, then let them be. Not everyone will reply, and that is normal.

## Day 5: Choose a target list

Instead of a long, vague list, pick around fifteen companies you would genuinely like to work for. For each, note:

- One person you could contact.
- One recent thing about the company (a product, a hire, a public announcement).
- Whether they have open roles that fit.

This list drives the next few weeks. It is fine to edit it as you learn more.

## Day 6: Practise out loud

Record yourself answering a few common questions:

- "Tell me about yourself."
- "Why did you leave?"
- "What is your biggest strength?"
- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager."

For "Why did you leave?", a short, neutral answer works: "My role was eliminated in a reorganisation. I am proud of [result], and I am looking for [what you want next]."

Listen back. Fix the answer that rambled the most.

## Day 7: Review and rest

- Count the conversations you have booked or started.
- Note what felt hardest this week.
- Set next week's goals: a handful of applications to roles where you already know someone, plus a few short calls.

Then take the rest of the day off. A job search is a long stretch of steady work, not a single sprint.

## What this week is not

It is not the week to accept the first offer out of panic, sign anything you have not read, or apply to a hundred roles with the same resume. If you have questions about severance or legal terms, a qualified professional is the right person to ask.
