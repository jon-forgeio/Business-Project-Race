# Resume Bullets That Show Impact: A Three-Part Rewrite You Can Do Tonight

Most resume bullets describe a job, not a person doing it. "Responsible for onboarding new clients" tells a reader what was on your list of duties. It does not tell them what happened because you were there.

The good news is that this is a writing problem, and writing problems have repeatable fixes. You do not need a new career story. You need a better sentence shape and a short session with your own memory.

## The shape: action, specifics, result

A bullet that shows impact usually has three parts:

1. **Action.** A verb you can defend in an interview: built, rewrote, cut, trained, negotiated, organised, automated, documented.
2. **Specifics.** What you worked on, and how big it was. Team size, volume, frequency, budget, number of people affected.
3. **Result.** What changed. Time saved, fewer errors, a deadline met, a process other people now use.

Here is the difference on an illustrative example:

- Before: "Responsible for the monthly reporting process."
- After: "Rebuilt the monthly reporting spreadsheet so the team could close the month in two days instead of four."

Nothing in the second version is exaggerated. It is just specific.

## Step 1: Collect raw material before you write

Do not start by editing the bullets you already have. Start with a blank page and answer these prompts for each recent role:

- What did people ask me for help with?
- What did I fix, rebuild or replace?
- What did I start that kept running after I stopped touching it?
- What did I make faster, cheaper, calmer or less error-prone?
- What did I teach someone else to do?

Write fast and messy. You are mining, not polishing.

## Step 2: Find a number, or an honest substitute

Numbers help a reader picture scale. But only use numbers you can explain if someone asks. If you do not have exact figures, you have options:

- **Use a range or an approximation you can stand behind**: "about 30 requests a week."
- **Use counts you know for certain**: number of people trained, documents written, vendors managed, locations supported.
- **Use before and after states**: "from a shared inbox with no owner to a tracked queue with a named owner per request."
- **Use time frames**: "within the first quarter," "every week for two years."

If the honest answer is "I do not know the number," a clear before and after is better than a guessed percentage.

## Step 3: Write three versions of each bullet

For each item from Step 1, write three short drafts:

- One that leads with the verb.
- One that leads with the result.
- One that is as short as you can make it.

Then pick the strongest, or combine parts. This takes a few minutes per bullet and almost always beats the first draft.

## Step 4: Cut the filler

Common filler words and phrases that can usually go:

- "Responsible for"
- "Helped to" (say what you actually did)
- "Various," "multiple," "a number of"
- "Successfully" (the result already shows success)
- "Team player," "detail-oriented," "results-driven" (show it, do not label it)

Read each bullet and ask: would this sentence fit on anyone's resume? If yes, it needs a specific detail.

## Step 5: Order bullets by what the next job cares about

Open the job posting you are targeting. Highlight the three things it asks for most clearly. Then reorder your bullets under each role so the most relevant one comes first. You are not changing facts, only emphasis.

## A quick self-check

Before you call a bullet finished, check that it:

- Starts with a verb.
- Names something concrete.
- Says what changed.
- Fits on one or two lines.
- Is something you could talk about for two minutes in an interview.

If a bullet fails two or more of these, rewrite it with the three-part shape.

## Keep a "wins file" from now on

The hardest part of writing good bullets is remembering what you did. Keep a running note, one line per week, of anything you fixed, shipped or improved. When you next update your resume, you will be editing, not excavating.
