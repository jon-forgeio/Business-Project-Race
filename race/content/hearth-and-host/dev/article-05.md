# How to Write a Wedding Toast That Is Short and Warm

Being asked to give a wedding toast is an honour, and it is also slightly terrifying. Most people stall because they try to say everything: every memory, every in-joke, every reason they love the couple. The result is long, and long toasts lose the room.

A toast works better when it is short and built around one story. Two to three minutes is plenty. Read aloud at a relaxed pace, that is roughly 300 to 400 words. Here is a structure that gets you there.

## Step 1: Find your one story

Answer each of these prompts in a single sentence. Do not polish anything yet.

- When did you first see how this person acts around their partner?
- What small habit of theirs would everyone in the room recognise?
- When did they surprise you by being kinder or braver than you expected?
- What went wrong once that turned out fine?

Now read your answers back. Circle the one that makes you smile while you read it. That is your story. The others can stay in your notebook.

A good test: can you tell it in under 40 seconds? If not, trim it down to the single moment that matters.

## Step 2: Use six parts

Fill in each part in plain language. Write it to be heard, not read.

**1. The opener.** One line that earns a laugh or a warm silence. It does not have to be a joke. "I have known [Name] for [time], and I have been waiting for this day for most of it" works fine.

**2. Who you are.** One sentence: your name and how you know the person. Half the room will not know you.

**3. The story.** Your circled story, with one concrete detail. A place, an object or a phrase someone said. Details make stories feel real.

**4. The turn.** One sentence that connects the story to the marriage. For example: "That is when I knew they would be good at this, because…"

**5. The partner.** Two sentences welcoming the partner. Mention one thing you have noticed about how the two of them fit together.

**6. The close.** Ask everyone to raise a glass, and name the couple.

That is the whole toast. Six parts, one story.

## Step 3: Cut it down

Once you have a draft, read it out loud with a timer.

- If it is over three minutes, cut. Start with background explanation; the room does not need it.
- Remove anything that needs a backstory to understand.
- Remove exes, money, embarrassing secrets, and anything you would not say in front of the couple's grandparents.
- Keep sentences short. Long sentences are hard to say and hard to follow.

## Step 4: A few openers to adapt

If you are stuck on the first line, borrow one of these and make it yours.

- "For those who do not know me, I am the person who knows too much and has promised to say too little."
- "I was asked to say a few words, which is risky, because I was also handed a microphone."
- "I wrote three versions of this: a funny one, a sentimental one and a short one. You are getting a mix."

## Step 5: Prepare for the day

- Print the toast in large type on a card. Phones are hard to read under bright lights, and they look like you are checking messages.
- Practise it out loud at least twice. Once alone, once to a friend.
- Ask that friend two questions: "Did the story make you smile?" and "Is there anything I should not say?"
- Go easy on drinks beforehand.
- Pause after anything funny. Let the room catch up.
- For the last line, look at the couple, not the paper.

## If you are nervous

Nerves are normal and they usually fade after the first sentence. A few things help:

- Know your opener and your close by heart. The middle can be read.
- Hold the card with both hands if they shake.
- Breathe before you start. Nobody minds a short pause.
- Remember that everyone in the room is on your side. They want the toast to go well.
