# Plan Your Fantasy Season Calendar Before Week One

Most commissioner stress comes from surprises: the trade deadline sneaks up, playoffs start and someone did not know, dues are still unpaid in week nine. Nearly all of these are calendar problems, and nearly all of them can be solved in about twenty minutes before the season starts.

Think of it like laying out a board game's turn track. Once every milestone has a square, everyone knows what comes next. Here is how to build that track for your league.

## Step 1: Pull the fixed dates

Start with the dates you do not control:

- The first week of the real sports season.
- The last regular-season week on your platform.
- The platform's default trade deadline, if it has one.
- Any bye weeks or schedule breaks that affect your sport.

Write these down first. Everything else hangs off them.

## Step 2: Choose your league's milestones

Now add the dates you do control. A typical friendly league has these:

| Milestone | When to set it |
|---|---|
| Rules posted | 2 to 3 weeks before the draft |
| Rules confirmed by everyone | 1 week before the draft |
| Draft | Usually the week before the season starts |
| Waivers open | Right after the draft or on a set day |
| Dues due (if any) | Before week 1 or before week 2 |
| Mid-season check-in | Around the halfway point |
| Trade deadline | A few weeks before playoffs |
| Playoff seeding locked | Last regular-season week |
| Playoffs | Final weeks of the season |
| Season wrap-up | Within a week of the final |

You can move any of these, but give each one an actual date.

## Step 3: Work backward from the playoffs

The playoffs are the most important stretch, so plan them first and fit the rest around them.

1. Decide how many teams make the playoffs.
2. Decide how many weeks the playoffs run.
3. Count back from the last week of your platform's season.
4. Put the trade deadline at least two to three weeks before the playoffs start, so rosters settle.

Many leagues avoid scheduling a championship in the very last week of a real sports season, when star players may rest. Check your sport's schedule and decide for yourself.

## Step 4: Add reminders, not just dates

A date on a calendar only helps the person who looks at it. Reminders turn dates into shared events. For each milestone, add two reminders to your own calendar:

- One a week before: "Post reminder: trade deadline next Wednesday."
- One the day before or the day of: "Post: trade deadline is tonight."

Write the reminder text in advance. On a busy week you will be glad you only have to copy and paste.

## Step 5: Schedule a mid-season check-in

Halfway through the season, post a short message:

> Halfway point! Quick check:
> 1. Any rule that felt unclear so far?
> 2. Anything you want changed for next season?
> 3. Reminder: trade deadline is [date].

You are not changing rules mid-season. You are collecting notes for next year and catching confusion early.

## Step 6: Plan the wrap-up

The season does not end at the final whistle. Put a wrap-up date on the calendar for:

- Announcing the champion.
- Settling any dues or prizes, following local laws for money or prizes.
- Posting a short "what we learned" list for next season.
- Asking who is in for next year.

Leagues that end cleanly tend to come back. Leagues that fade out in week seventeen often do not.

## Step 7: Publish the calendar

Post the whole calendar as one message and pin it next to your rules. A plain list is fine:

```
SEASON CALENDAR
Rules posted: ____
Rules confirmed: ____
Draft: ____
Waivers open: ____
Dues due: ____
Mid-season check-in: ____
Trade deadline: ____
Playoffs: ____ to ____
Championship: ____
Wrap-up: ____
```

Every date answers a future question before anyone asks it.

## A weekly rhythm to go with it

The calendar covers the big moments. A weekly rhythm covers the rest:

- **Early week:** post a two- or three-line recap of the previous week.
- **Midweek:** waiver reminder.
- **Before the first game:** lineup reminder if you have had forgotten lineups.

Keep it light. The goal is a league that feels organized, not a newsletter.
