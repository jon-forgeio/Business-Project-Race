# How to Write Fantasy League Rules People Actually Read

Every friendly league has a rulebook. Most of them share the same fate: a long document gets posted in the group chat before the draft, a few people open it, nobody reads past the first screen, and the first real argument of the season starts with "wait, where does it say that?"

The fix is not more rules. It is better-shaped rules. Think of your rulebook like a board game's quick-reference card: the stuff you need mid-game is on the front, and the edge cases live in the back of the box. Here is a method you can use this week.

## Step 1: Split rules into "every week" and "once in a while"

Grab your current rules (or a blank page) and sort every line into two piles.

- **Every week:** lineup lock time, waiver order and timing, how trades get approved, how ties in a weekly matchup are settled.
- **Once in a while:** what happens if a manager leaves mid-season, how keepers work, what happens if the hosting platform changes a setting.

The "every week" pile goes on page one. The "once in a while" pile goes in an appendix. Most managers will only ever need page one, so make page one excellent.

## Step 2: Write each rule as one sentence with one number

Long rules hide decisions inside paragraphs. Short rules expose them. Aim for one sentence per rule, and wherever possible, put a concrete number or time in it.

Instead of:

> Trades will be reviewed by the league to ensure fairness and may be vetoed if deemed problematic.

Try:

> Trades process after a 24-hour review window. A trade is reversed only if the commissioner finds clear collusion and explains why in the league chat.

The second version answers the questions people actually ask: how long, who decides, and what counts.

## Step 3: Number your rules

Numbers turn arguments into references. "Rule 4 says lineups lock at kickoff of each game" ends a debate faster than "I think we agreed on something like that." Use a simple scheme:

1. Roster and lineups
2. Waivers and free agents
3. Trades
4. Scoring and tiebreakers
5. Playoffs
6. Conduct and disputes

Keep the numbering stable from season to season so returning managers can find things.

## Step 4: Add a "What changed" box at the top

Returning managers skim. They assume last year's rules still apply. A three-line box at the very top solves that:

> **Changed this season:**
> - Waivers now run Wednesday morning instead of Tuesday night.
> - Playoffs expand from four teams to six.
> - Trade deadline moves one week earlier.

If nothing changed, say so. "No rule changes this season" is useful information too.

## Step 5: Write the default for every gap

No rulebook covers everything. What saves you is a fallback rule, written down before anyone needs it. Something like:

> If a situation is not covered here, the hosting platform's default setting applies. If the platform has no setting, the commissioner decides and adds a rule for next season.

That one sentence prevents a lot of improvised rulings that feel unfair after the fact.

## Step 6: Get a quick "I read it" from everyone

Before the draft, ask each manager to reply with a thumbs-up or a one-word confirmation that they read page one. It takes them thirty seconds and it changes the tone of every later disagreement. People are much calmer about rules they confirmed than about rules they "never saw."

## Step 7: Pin it in one place

Pick a single home for the rules: a pinned message, a shared doc, or the league notes field on your platform. Not all three. When there are three copies, eventually there are three versions.

## A one-page skeleton you can copy

```
LEAGUE RULES (Season ____)

Changed this season:
- 

1. Lineups lock: ____
2. Waivers run: ____ / order: ____
3. Trades: review window ____ / reversed only if ____
4. Weekly tie: settled by ____
5. Playoffs: ____ teams, weeks ____ to ____
6. Disputes: raise within ____ hours, commissioner replies within ____

Not covered here? Platform default applies, then commissioner decides.
```

Fill in the blanks, post it, and collect your thumbs-ups. You now have a rulebook that fits on one screen.
