# Start with the prompt's verb
Scholarship prompts usually ask you to do one of four things: describe, explain, reflect, or show why. Underline the verb first. "Describe a challenge" asks for a scene. "Reflect on a challenge" asks what you learned. Writing the wrong kind of essay is the most common reason a strong story scores poorly.

# A two-minute story test
Before you outline, answer these in a note, in plain sentences:
- What happened, with a date or a rough time frame?
- What did you do, specifically?
- What changed because of it, for you or for someone else?

If any answer is vague, you do not have an essay yet. Keep asking questions until each answer has a fact in it.

# Worked example (illustrative)
Prompt: "Describe a challenge you have overcome."

- Scene: The first week at a new job, I had to close the monthly report alone because my supervisor was on leave.
- Action: I found the three spreadsheets that fed it, asked the accounts team for the one formula nobody had documented, and wrote a checklist for next time.
- Change: The report went out one day late instead of not at all, and the checklist is still used.
- Lesson: Asking one specific question early saved more time than working quietly for another day.

Notice the outline has a scene, a decision, a result and a lesson. Your own story will look different, and that is the point.

# Three common mistakes
- Listing achievements without one scene that shows how you think.
- Spending most of the essay on the problem and rushing the change.
- Writing about a person who influenced you without saying what you did with their influence.
