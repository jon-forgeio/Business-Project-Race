# The shape of a good toast
A toast is 2 to 3 minutes: about 300 to 400 words. People remember one story and one feeling, not a list of facts. Build the toast around a single story.
# Step 1: pick your story
Answer each in one sentence, then circle the one that makes you smile while you write it.
- A moment when you first saw how this person acts around their partner.
- A small, specific habit everyone in the room recognises.
- A time they surprised you by being kinder or braver than you expected.
- Something that went wrong and turned out fine.
The best story is one you can tell in under 40 seconds. Cut the rest.
# Step 2: the six parts
- **Opener.** One line that earns a laugh or a warm silence.
- **Who you are.** Your name and how you know the person, in one sentence.
- **The story.** Your circled story, with one concrete detail (a place, an object, a phrase).
- **The turn.** One sentence tying the story to the marriage: "That is when I knew they would be good at this because..."
- **The partner.** Two sentences welcoming the partner. Mention one thing you noticed about how the two fit together.
- **The close.** Raise your glass.
# Openers worth adapting
- "For those who do not know me, I am the person who knows too much and has promised to say too little."
- "I was asked to say a few words, which is dangerous because I was also handed a microphone."
- "[Name] once told me they would never get married. I am delighted to report they were wrong."
- "I prepared three speeches: a funny one, a sentimental one, and a short one. You are getting a combination."
# Delivery checklist
- Time yourself out loud. Cut anything past three minutes.
- Print it in large type on a card. Phones are hard to read in a bright room.
- Go easy on the drinks beforehand.
- Pause after jokes. Look at the couple for the last line.
- Avoid exes, money, embarrassing secrets, and anything that needs explaining.
- Ask one friend: "Did the story make you smile? Is there anything you would not say in front of the grandparents?"
